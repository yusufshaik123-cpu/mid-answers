Series Circuit Analysis
For resistors R₁, R₂, R₃ connected in SERIES: R_eq = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ Example: R₁ = 10Ω, R₂ = 20Ω, R₃ = 30Ω, V = 60V R_eq = 10 + 20 + 30 = 60Ω I_total = V / R_eq = 60 / 60 = 1 A Voltage drops: V₁ = I × R₁ = 1 × 10 = 10V V₂ = I × R₂ = 1 × 20 = 20V V₃ = I × R₃ = 1 × 30 = 30V Check: V₁ + V₂ + V₃ = 10 + 20 + 30 = 60V ✓
Parallel Circuit Analysis
For resistors R₁, R₂, R₃ connected in PARALLEL: 1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ Example: R₁ = 10Ω, R₂ = 20Ω, R₃ = 30Ω, V = 60V 1/R_eq = 1/10 + 1/20 + 1/30 = 6/60 + 3/60 + 2/60 = 11/60 R_eq = 60/11 ≈ 5.45Ω I_total = V / R_eq = 60 / 5.45 ≈ 11 A Branch currents: I₁ = V/R₁ = 60/10 = 6A I₂ = V/R₂ = 60/20 = 3A I₃ = V/R₃ = 60/30 = 2A I_total = 6 + 3 + 2 = 11A ✓
Part (b): Power Factor and its Significance
Definition of Power Factor

Power Factor (PF) is the ratio of Active (Real) Power (P) consumed by a circuit to the Apparent Power (S) supplied to the circuit.

Power Factor = P / S = cos φ
Where: P = Active Power (Watts, W) — useful work done S = Apparent Power (Volt-Amperes, VA) — total power supplied Q = Reactive Power (VAR) — stored/returned power Power Factor = cos φ (dimensionless, ranges from 0 to 1) φ = phase angle between voltage and current
Power Factor Triangle
S (Apparent Power, VA) │╲ │ ╲ Q │ ╲ S = √(P² + Q²) │ ╲ └────────╲ P (Real Power, W) cos φ = P/S (Power Factor) sin φ = Q/S tan φ = Q/P
Significance of Power Factor
  • Unity PF (cos φ = 1): Pure resistive load — all power is consumed as useful work (ideal condition).
  • Zero PF (cos φ = 0): Pure reactive load — no real power consumed, all power is reactive.
  • Low PF (0 to 0.8): More current drawn for the same real power → increased I²R losses in transmission lines.
  • Economic Significance: Electricity boards penalize industries with low PF (below 0.85). Higher PF reduces electricity bills.
  • Equipment Rating: Generators, transformers, cables are rated in kVA. Low PF means less kW output for same kVA capacity.
  • Power Factor Correction: Achieved by connecting capacitor banks in parallel for inductive loads (motors, transformers).
✅
R_eq(series) = ΣR; R_eq(parallel) = 1/Σ(1/R); I = V/R_eq. Power Factor = cos φ = P/S; Low PF increases losses and electricity cost; corrected using capacitors.

Part (a) & (b): Kirchhoff's Laws
Fig: KCL – Sum of currents at a node
Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) — Definition

Statement: The algebraic sum of all currents entering and leaving a junction (node) is zero.

ΣI_entering = ΣI_leaving     OR     ΣI = 0 at any node

Based on the Law of Conservation of Charge — charge cannot accumulate at a node.

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) — Definition

Statement: The algebraic sum of all voltages (EMFs and voltage drops) around any closed loop is zero.

ΣV = 0    (around any closed loop)

Based on the Law of Conservation of Energy — energy gained from EMF sources equals energy lost in resistors.

Numerical — Applying KVL (Single Loop)
Circuit: Battery E = 20V, R₁ = 4Ω, R₂ = 6Ω in series Applying KVL (clockwise loop): +E − I×R₁ − I×R₂ = 0 20 − 4I − 6I = 0 20 = 10I I = 2 A Voltage drops: V_R1 = I × R₁ = 2 × 4 = 8V V_R2 = I × R₂ = 2 × 6 = 12V Verification: 8 + 12 = 20V = E ✓ (KVL verified)
Numerical — Applying KCL (Junction with 3 branches)
At node A, three branches meet: I₁ = 6A (entering) I₂ = 4A (entering) I₃ = ? (leaving) Applying KCL: ΣI_entering = ΣI_leaving I₁ + I₂ = I₃ 6 + 4 = I₃ I₃ = 10 A (leaving the node) Check: ΣI = 6 + 4 − 10 = 0 ✓ (KCL verified)
Verification in a Two-Mesh Network
Two-mesh circuit: Mesh 1: E₁ = 12V, R₁ = 2Ω (shared: R₃ = 4Ω) Mesh 2: E₂ = 6V, R₂ = 2Ω (shared: R₃ = 4Ω) Let mesh currents: I₁ (clockwise in mesh 1), I₂ (clockwise in mesh 2) KVL for Mesh 1: 12 − 2I₁ − 4(I₁ − I₂) = 0 12 − 6I₁ + 4I₂ = 0 ...(1) KVL for Mesh 2: 6 − 2I₂ − 4(I₂ − I₁) = 0 6 − 6I₂ + 4I₁ = 0 ...(2) Solving: I₁ = 2A, I₂ = 1.67A Current in shared resistor R₃ = I₁ − I₂ = 0.33A KCL at junction: 2A in, 1.67A + 0.33A = 2A out ✓
✅
KCL: ΣI_in = ΣI_out at any node. KVL: ΣV = 0 around any closed loop. Both verified numerically.

Part (a): Ohm's Law and its Limitations
Fig: Ohm's Law – Linear V-I Characteristic
Statement of Ohm's Law

Statement: At constant temperature, the current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference (voltage) applied across it.

V = I × R     OR     I = V/R     OR     R = V/I
Where: V = Voltage (Volts, V) I = Current (Amperes, A) R = Resistance (Ohms, Ω) — constant of proportionality Example: V = 12V, R = 4Ω → I = 12/4 = 3A
Limitations of Ohm's Law
  • Temperature Dependence: Valid only at constant temperature. Resistance of metals increases with temperature, so the law doesn't apply strictly when temperature varies.
  • Non-linear Devices: Does not apply to non-ohmic (unilateral) devices like diodes, transistors, thermistors, and LDRs where V-I is not linear.
  • Electrolytes: Does not apply to electrolytic solutions — current causes chemical changes that alter resistance.
  • Arc discharge & gas discharge tubes: Current varies non-linearly with voltage.
  • Semiconductors: Silicon, germanium do not obey Ohm's law — resistance changes with voltage and current magnitude.
  • Magnetic materials: When resistance changes due to magnetic effects, the linear relationship breaks down.

Part (b): AC Terminologies
Fig: AC sinusoidal waveform showing key parameters
(i) Time Period (T)

Definition: The time taken for one complete cycle of an alternating quantity.

T = 1/f     (seconds)

For Indian supply (50 Hz): T = 1/50 = 0.02 seconds = 20 ms

(ii) Frequency (f)

Definition: The number of complete cycles completed per second by an alternating quantity.

f = 1/T     (Hertz, Hz)

India: 50 Hz; USA: 60 Hz. Higher frequency → faster oscillation.

(iii) Amplitude (Peak Value, Vm or Im)

Definition: The maximum value reached by the alternating quantity in either the positive or negative half-cycle.

v(t) = V_m sin(ωt)  →  Amplitude = V_m

For 230V supply: V_m = 230 × √2 ≈ 325.27V

(iv) Peak-to-Peak Value (Vpp)

Definition: The total voltage variation from the maximum positive peak to the maximum negative peak.

V_pp = 2 × V_m

For 230V RMS supply: V_pp = 2 × 325.27 ≈ 650.54V

✅
i_x found using current divider / KVL. T = 1/f; f = cycles/sec; Amplitude = peak value V_m; Peak-to-Peak = 2V_m.

Part (b): Power Triangle
Fig: Power Triangle — Active (P), Reactive (Q) and Apparent Power (S)
Power Triangle Explanation
The power triangle represents the relationship between the three types of electrical power in an AC circuit: S (kVA) │╲ │ ╲ Q │ ╲ S = √(P² + Q²) (kVAR)│ ╲ └────────╲ P (kW) φ = phase angle ──────────────────────────────────────────── TERM SYMBOL UNIT FORMULA ──────────────────────────────────────────── Active Power P kW P = VIcosφ = I²R Reactive Power Q kVAR Q = VIsinφ = I²X Apparent Power S kVA S = VI = √(P² + Q²) Power Factor PF none PF = P/S = cosφ ────────────────────────────────────────────
Definitions of Each Term
  • Active Power (P): Real power actually consumed and converted to useful work (heat, light, mechanical). Unit: Watt (W) or kW.
  • Reactive Power (Q): Power oscillating between source and reactive components (L or C). Not consumed but reduces system efficiency. Unit: VAR or kVAR.
  • Apparent Power (S): Total power supplied by the source = product of RMS voltage and RMS current. Unit: VA or kVA.
  • Power Factor (PF): Ratio P/S = cosφ. Ranges 0–1. Unity = pure resistive. Low PF = wasteful.

Part (b): Derivation of RMS and Average Values
RMS Value — Definition & Derivation

RMS (Root Mean Square): The equivalent DC value that produces the same heating effect as the AC in a purely resistive circuit.

For v(t) = V_m sin(ωt), over one full period T = 2π/ω: V_rms = √[ (1/T) ∫₀ᵀ v²(t) dt ] Step 1: v²(t) = V_m² sin²(ωt) = V_m² × (1 − cos 2ωt)/2 Step 2: (1/T) ∫₀ᵀ V_m²/2 × (1 − cos 2ωt) dt Step 3: = V_m²/2 × [t − sin(2ωt)/(2ω)]₀ᵀ = V_m²/2 × [T − 0] / T = V_m²/2 Step 4: V_rms = √(V_m²/2) = V_m / √2 ≈ 0.707 V_m ∴ V_rms = V_m / √2
Average Value — Definition & Derivation

Average Value: The mean value of all instantaneous values over a half cycle (for a symmetric AC wave, full-cycle average = 0).

For v(t) = V_m sin(ωt), over half period (0 to π): V_avg = (1/(π/ω)) ∫₀^(π/ω) V_m sin(ωt) dt = (ω/π) × V_m × [−cos(ωt)/ω]₀^(π/ω) = (V_m/π) × [−cos(π) + cos(0)] = (V_m/π) × [−(−1) + 1] = (V_m/π) × 2 ∴ V_avg = 2V_m / π ≈ 0.637 V_m
✅
V_m=10V, f=0.637Hz, T=1.571s, V_rms=7.071V, V_avg=6.366V, φ=+45°. Derivation: V_rms = V_m/√2 ≈ 0.707V_m; V_avg = 2V_m/π ≈ 0.637V_m.

Part (a): DC Generator
Key Principle: Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction — "When a conductor moves in a magnetic field, an EMF is induced in it."
EMF equation: E = ΦZNP/60A   where Φ=flux/pole, Z=conductors, N=RPM, P=poles, A=parallel paths
Fig: DC Generator – Construction and Working
What is an Electrical Machine?

An Electrical Machine is a device that converts energy from one form to another using electromagnetic principles:

  • Generator: Converts mechanical energy → Electrical energy
  • Motor: Converts electrical energy → Mechanical energy
  • Transformer: Transfers electrical energy between circuits (changes voltage/current levels)
Construction of DC Generator
  • Stator (Field System): Consists of poles with field windings that produce the magnetic flux. Yoke (outer frame) provides mechanical support.
  • Armature (Rotor): Rotating cylindrical core made of laminated iron. Carries armature conductors (windings) in slots.
  • Commutator: Segmented copper cylinder fitted on the armature shaft. Converts alternating induced EMF in conductors to unidirectional (DC) output.
  • Brushes: Carbon brushes maintain sliding electrical contact with commutator segments to collect current.
Working Principle of DC Generator
1. The field windings carry DC to create a strong magnetic field (N and S poles). 2. The armature is rotated mechanically by a prime mover (engine/turbine). 3. Armature conductors cut the magnetic flux lines → EMF is induced (Faraday's Law: e = B × l × v × sin θ) 4. The induced EMF is alternating (AC) in the armature conductors. 5. The commutator rectifies the AC to DC at the output terminals. 6. Carbon brushes collect this DC current and supply to external circuit. EMF equation: E = (ΦZNP) / (60A) Φ = flux per pole (Wb) Z = total armature conductors N = speed (rpm) P = number of poles A = number of parallel paths (= 2 for wave winding, = P for lap)
Part (b): Megger — High Resistance Insulation Tester
Fig: Megger – used for measuring insulation resistance
What is a Megger?

A Megger (Megaohmmeter) is an instrument used to measure very high electrical resistances — particularly the insulation resistance of cables, motors, transformers, and other equipment. It applies a high DC voltage (500V, 1000V, or 2500V) and measures the leakage current to determine resistance in Megaohms (MΩ).

Construction and Operation of Megger
Components: 1. Hand-operated DC generator (or battery + DC-DC converter in digital types) – Generates 500V or 1000V DC. 2. Two coils (Current Coil & Pressure/Voltage Coil) — cross-coil type movement. 3. Deflecting coil connected across measured resistance R_x. 4. Control coil (connected in series with a fixed resistor R_s) limits current. Working: ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ Generator → High Voltage DC │ │ Current flows: Generator → Line Terminal │ │ → Cable/Machine │ │ → Earth Terminal │ │ → Generator │ └─────────────────────────────────────────────┘ Pointer deflection: • Torque by current coil ∝ current through R_x (= V/R_x) • Torque by pressure coil ∝ voltage V • At balance: Pointer indicates R_x in MΩ on the scale Scale Readings: ∞ MΩ → Perfect insulation (no leakage) 0 MΩ → Dead short (complete breakdown) >1 MΩ → Acceptable insulation <0.5 MΩ → Insulation needs replacement
✅
DC Generator: Faraday's law → rotating armature in magnetic field → EMF = ΦZNP/60A → commutator converts AC to DC. Megger: applies high DC voltage, measures leakage current → gives insulation resistance in MΩ.

Part (a): Back EMF vs Generated EMF in DC Motor
Generated EMF (E_g) — in DC Generator

Generated EMF is the EMF induced in the armature conductors when the armature rotates in a magnetic field (generator action).

E_g = ΦZNP / (60A)

Function: Drives current through the external load circuit. It is the source EMF in a generator.

Back EMF (E_b) — in DC Motor

Back EMF (Counter EMF) is the EMF induced in the rotating armature of a DC motor that opposes the applied supply voltage (Lenz's Law).

V = E_b + I_a × R_a  →  E_b = V − I_a R_a

Function:

  • Self-regulates motor speed — more load → speed drops → E_b decreases → more current → more torque (self-compensating).
  • Limits armature current and prevents motor burnout.
  • Acts as an internal energy conversion controller.

Part (b): Single Phase Induction Motor — Not Self-Starting
Why Single Phase IM is NOT Self-Starting
A single-phase supply produces a PULSATING magnetic field (not rotating). This pulsating field can be resolved into: F = F_forward + F_backward (two equal, opposite rotating fields) At standstill (N = 0): • Forward torque T_f = backward torque T_b • Net torque = T_f − T_b = 0 • Motor CANNOT start on its own. Once given a push in either direction: • That direction's torque becomes dominant • Motor continues and accelerates ∴ We need an external mechanism to produce initial starting torque.
Type 1 — Split-Phase Induction Motor
Fig: Split-Phase Induction Motor
Construction: Main winding: Low resistance, high inductance (placed in stator slots) Auxiliary (starting) winding: High resistance, low inductance Working: • Two currents (I_main and I_aux) are out of phase by ~30° • This creates a rotating magnetic field → starting torque produced • Centrifugal switch disconnects auxiliary winding at ~75% full speed • Motor continues on main winding alone Applications: Fans, blowers, washing machines, small compressors
Type 2 — Capacitor-Start Induction Motor
Fig: Capacitor-Start Induction Motor
Construction: Main winding + Auxiliary winding in series with CAPACITOR Centrifugal switch disconnects auxiliary circuit after starting Working: • Capacitor causes auxiliary current to LEAD by ~90° • Phase difference between I_main and I_aux ≈ 90° • Better rotating field → Higher starting torque than split-phase • At 75% speed: centrifugal switch opens, auxiliary circuit disconnected Advantage over split-phase: Starting torque = 3–4 times full load torque (much higher) Applications: Compressors, pumps, refrigerators, air conditioners
✅
Back EMF = V − I_aR_a; regulates motor speed. 1-phase IM not self-starting due to zero net torque at standstill (pulsating field). Split-phase and capacitor-start motors create artificial 2-phase supply for starting torque.

Role in Power System

The transformer is the backbone of AC power transmission and distribution. India uses a typical power system hierarchy:

Generating Station → Step-Up Transformer (11kV → 220kV/400kV) → Transmission Lines (High Voltage, Low Current → Less I²R loss) → Step-Down Transformer (220kV → 33kV → 11kV → 415V/230V) → Industrial/Domestic Load Reason: Power P = VI = constant (for ideal transformer) At high voltage V: current I is small → transmission loss I²R is small Power loss without transformer: P_loss ∝ I² (very high at generation voltage) Power loss with transformer: P_loss ∝ (I/k)² = I²/k² (k² times less)
Working Principle — Mutual Induction
Principle: Faraday's Law of Mutual Induction When AC flows in primary winding: 1. Alternating magnetic flux φ is set up in the core 2. This flux links with secondary winding 3. EMF is induced in secondary (e₂ = −N₂ dφ/dt) Transformer Equations: E₁/E₂ = N₁/N₂ = V₁/V₂ (voltage ratio) I₁/I₂ = N₂/N₁ (current ratio — inverse) N₁I₁ = N₂I₂ (Ampere-turn balance) For ideal transformer: V₁I₁ = V₂I₂ (Input power = Output power) Types: Step-Up Transformer: N₂ > N₁ → V₂ > V₁ (increases voltage) Step-Down Transformer: N₂ < N₁ → V₂ < V₁ (decreases voltage)

Part (a): Role of Commutator in DC Machines
Fig: Commutator – converts AC to DC in generator; DC to AC in motor
Role in DC Generator

In a DC generator, the armature conductors generate alternating EMF as they rotate through North and South poles. The commutator acts as a mechanical rectifier:

  • Commutator segments are connected to armature conductors and rotate with the armature.
  • Stationary carbon brushes contact commutator segments and reverse their connections every half revolution.
  • This reversal ensures current always flows in the same direction in the external circuit → unidirectional (DC) output.
Role in DC Motor

In a DC motor, the commutator acts as a mechanical inverter:

  • DC supply is fed through brushes to commutator segments.
  • Commutator reverses the direction of current in armature conductors every half rotation.
  • This ensures the torque always acts in the same direction, causing continuous rotation.
  • Without commutator, the torque would reverse every half turn and the motor would oscillate but not rotate continuously.
Part (b): Wheatstone Bridge — Medium Resistance Measurement
Fig: Wheatstone Bridge – balanced condition gives unknown resistance
Wheatstone Bridge — Principle and Construction
The Wheatstone Bridge measures MEDIUM resistances (1Ω to 100kΩ) accurately. Circuit: Four resistors P, Q, R, X in diamond arrangement • P, Q = Known ratio arms • R = Variable (adjustable) known resistance • X = Unknown resistance to be measured • G = Galvanometer (null detector) connected between B and D • Battery E connected between A and C A / \ P Q / \ B---G---D \ / R X \ / C | E (Battery)
Derivation of Balance Condition
At BALANCE: No current through galvanometer (I_G = 0) This means: V_B = V_D (potentials at B and D are equal) Voltage across P = Voltage across Q (from current I₁ through P-R arm) Voltage across R = Voltage across X (from current I₂ through Q-X arm) From P-R branch: I₁ = E / (P + R) V_B = E × R / (P + R) ... (potential at B w.r.t. C) From Q-X branch: I₂ = E / (Q + X) V_D = E × X / (Q + X) ... (potential at D w.r.t. C) At balance: V_B = V_D E × R / (P + R) = E × X / (Q + X) R(Q + X) = X(P + R) RQ + RX = XP + XR RQ = XP ∴ X = (P/Q) × R ← BALANCE CONDITION (Unknown R formula) OR: P/Q = X/R → P × X = Q × R (cross products equal)
Procedure for Measurement
1. Connect unknown X in one arm 2. Choose ratio P/Q (e.g., 1:1, 1:10, 10:1) 3. Vary R until galvanometer reads ZERO (null/balance condition) 4. At balance: X = (P/Q) × R 5. Read value of R → Calculate X Advantages: • High accuracy (error < 0.1%) • Not affected by EMF of battery • Simple null method Range: 1Ω to about 100kΩ (medium resistance)
✅
Commutator: Generator → rectifies AC to DC; Motor → reverses current for continuous torque. Wheatstone Bridge: at balance P×X = Q×R → X = (P/Q)×R. Used for medium resistance (1Ω–100kΩ).

Part (a): PMMC Instrument — Permanent Magnet Moving Coil
Fig: PMMC Instrument – Permanent Magnet Moving Coil meter
Construction of PMMC
  • Permanent Magnet: Horse-shoe (U-shaped) magnet provides strong, uniform radial magnetic field in air gap.
  • Moving Coil: Rectangular aluminum former wound with fine copper wire, pivoted on jewel bearings inside air gap.
  • Iron Core (Cylindrical): Fixed soft iron cylinder inside coil; concentrates and makes field uniform and radial.
  • Control Springs (Hair springs): Two phosphor-bronze springs provide controlling torque proportional to deflection. Also serve as current paths to coil.
  • Pointer & Scale: Light aluminum pointer attached to coil; moves over calibrated scale.
  • Damping: Eddy current damping — induced currents in aluminum former oppose motion and prevent oscillation.
Working Principle of PMMC
Principle: A current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences a torque. Deflecting Torque: T_d = N × B × A × I N = number of turns in coil B = flux density in air gap (Tesla) A = area of coil (m²) I = current (A) T_d ∝ I (directly proportional to current) Controlling Torque (springs): T_c = k × θ (k = spring constant, θ = deflection angle) At equilibrium: T_d = T_c N × B × A × I = k × θ θ = (NBA/k) × I θ ∝ I ∴ Scale is LINEAR (uniform spacing) — deflection proportional to current For DC: Pointer deflects in correct direction. For AC: Average torque = 0 (reverses every half cycle) → pointer doesn't move. ∴ PMMC works for DC ONLY.

Part (b): Electric Shock — Safety Precautions
What is Electric Shock?

Electric shock is the physiological reaction or injury caused by electric current passing through the human body. It occurs when a person becomes part of an electrical circuit — current flows in through one point and out through another (usually ground).

Effects of Current on Body (50Hz AC): 1 mA : Slight tingling sensation (threshold of perception) 5 mA : Painful shock, but can let go 10–20 mA : Muscular contractions — "can't let go" current 50–100 mA : Ventricular fibrillation, potentially fatal 100–200 mA : Cardiac arrest, severe burns, death likely >200 mA : Severe burns, charring (current may bypass heart)
Safety Precautions to Avoid Electric Shock
  • 1. Earthing/Grounding: All metallic parts of equipment must be properly earthed. Leakage current flows to earth instead of through a person.
  • 2. Use of Fuses and MCBs: Overcurrent protection devices disconnect supply when current exceeds safe limits.
  • 3. ELCB / RCCB: Earth Leakage Circuit Breaker trips within 30ms when leakage current exceeds 30mA — life-saving device.
  • 4. Insulation: All live conductors and wires must have proper insulation (PVC or rubber). Regularly inspect for worn insulation.
  • 5. Isolation Before Work: Always switch off and lock out the supply (LOTO) before working on electrical equipment.
  • 6. Use of PPE: Wear rubber-soled boots, insulated gloves, and safety helmets when working on electrical systems.
  • 7. Dry Conditions: Never operate electrical equipment with wet hands or in wet/damp surroundings — water greatly reduces body resistance.
  • 8. Double Insulation: Portable appliances should have double insulation (Class II appliances — no earthing pin needed).
  • 9. Safe Working Distance: Maintain safe distances from high-voltage lines as per electrical safety regulations.
  • 10. First Aid: Don't touch a shocked person directly — switch off supply first or use non-conducting object to push them away. Then provide CPR.
✅

Principle & Components of Wind Power Plant

Principle: Kinetic energy of wind → Mechanical rotation of blades → Electrical energy via generator. Power P = ½ρAv³ (watts), where ρ=air density, A=swept area, v=wind speed.

COMPONENTS: ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── 1. ROTOR BLADES (2 or 3): Large aerofoil-shaped blades (20–80m each). Lift force due to wind → rotational torque on hub. Material: Fiberglass reinforced epoxy (light, strong). 2. HUB: Central part connecting blades to main shaft. Pitch control mechanism adjusts blade angle for optimal efficiency. 3. LOW SPEED SHAFT: Rotates at 10–25 RPM (same as rotor). Connected to gearbox input. 4. GEARBOX: Increases rotational speed from ~20 RPM to ~1500 RPM. Required because generators need high speed for efficient power. (Some modern turbines use direct-drive permanent magnet generators — no gearbox.) 5. HIGH SPEED SHAFT & BRAKE: Connects gearbox output to generator. Mechanical brake for emergency stop or maintenance. 6. GENERATOR: Converts mechanical rotation to 3-phase AC electricity. Types: Induction generator (most common) or PMSG. Output: Variable frequency AC (then converted by power electronics). 7. NACELLE: Housing that encloses gearbox, generator, and control systems. Mounted on top of tower; rotates to face wind (yaw control). 8. YAW MECHANISM: Rotates nacelle + rotor to face into the wind direction. Driven by yaw motors controlled by wind direction sensors (vane). 9. ANEMOMETER & WIND VANE: Anemometer: Measures wind speed → controls pitch and brake. Wind vane: Measures wind direction → controls yaw. 10. TOWER: Steel tubular or lattice structure (50–120m height). Higher tower = stronger, more consistent wind. 11. POWER ELECTRONICS (Converter): Converts variable frequency AC to fixed 50Hz grid-compatible AC. Feeds into step-up transformer and then to national grid. 12. TRANSFORMER: Steps up generator output to transmission voltage (11kV or 33kV). 13. FOUNDATION: Concrete base anchoring tower to ground (or seabed for offshore).
Part (b): Fuse and MCB — Operation
Fig: Fuses and MCBs – overcurrent protection devices
Fuse — Operation
FUSE: The oldest and simplest overcurrent protection device. Construction: • Thin conductor (tin, lead, or tin-lead alloy wire) enclosed in a ceramic/glass body with end caps. • Rated current: minimum current at which fuse operates. • Breaking capacity: maximum fault current fuse can interrupt safely. Working Principle: • Normal condition: Current < rating → fuse wire stays intact. • Overcurrent/fault: I > I_rating → I²R heating melts fuse wire. • Circuit is broken → fault is isolated. Characteristics: • Pre-arcing time: Time from fault to start of melting. • Arcing time: Time for arc to extinguish after melting. • Total operating time = Pre-arcing + Arcing time Limitations: ✗ Cannot be reset — must replace after operation (single-use). ✗ Slow response (seconds to minutes) for small overcurrents. ✗ Cannot distinguish between overload and short circuit precisely.
MCB (Miniature Circuit Breaker) — Operation
MCB: Modern, resettable overcurrent protection device. Construction: 1. Bimetallic Strip (thermal element): For overload protection. 2. Electromagnetic Solenoid (magnetic element): For short-circuit protection. 3. Trip mechanism and ON/OFF toggle. 4. Arc chute: Extinguishes arc when contacts open. Working — Overload Protection (Bimetallic Strip): • Two metals (brass + invar) bonded together expand at different rates. • Overcurrent → heating → bimetallic strip bends → trips mechanism. • Operates in seconds to minutes (time-delay) for sustained overloads. Working — Short Circuit Protection (Solenoid): • High fault current → strong magnetic field in solenoid. • Solenoid pulls plunger → instantly trips contacts open. • Operates in milliseconds (<10ms) for large fault currents. Advantages over Fuse: ✓ Resettable — flip back ON after fault is cleared. ✓ Faster operation for short circuits. ✓ Can be used as a switch. ✓ Visual indication of tripped state (handle in middle). ✓ More reliable — factory calibrated. MCB Types (by tripping curve): Type B: Trips at 3–5 × rated current (resistive loads) Type C: Trips at 5–10 × rated current (motors, fluorescent lights) Type D: Trips at 10–20 × rated current (transformers, large motors)
✅
Wind plant: Blades→Gearbox→Generator→Power electronics→Transformer→Grid. Fuse: melts on overcurrent (single-use, slow). MCB: bimetal for overload + solenoid for short circuit (resettable, fast).

Part (b): Types of Earthing
Fig: Plate and Pipe Earthing – connecting metallic parts to ground
Purpose of Earthing

Earthing (Grounding) is the connection of non-current-carrying metallic parts of electrical equipment to the earth (ground) through a low-resistance conductor. Purpose: protect persons from electric shock by providing a safe path for fault/leakage current to flow to earth, thereby operating the protection device (fuse/MCB).

Types of Earthing
1. PLATE EARTHING: Construction: Copper plate (60cm×60cm×3.18mm) or GI plate (60cm×60cm×6.35mm) buried ≥3m deep in moist soil. Surrounding: Alternate layers of charcoal and salt increase conductivity. Water pipe: Pour water regularly to keep soil moist. Earth resistance: < 1Ω for copper, < 2Ω for GI Use: Suitable for areas with stable soil conditions. 2. PIPE EARTHING (Most Common in India): Construction: GI pipe (38mm diameter, 2–3m long) driven vertically into moist soil. Top 20cm: GI funnel to pour water for maintenance. Surrounding: Salt and charcoal layers (same as plate earthing). Diameter and length depend on soil resistivity. Advantage: Easy to install, low cost, widely used for domestic/industrial. 3. ROD EARTHING: Construction: Copper-clad steel rod (12–25mm diameter, 1–3m long) driven into the ground. Used in areas with hard rock or low soil moisture. Multiple rods connected in parallel for lower resistance. 4. STRIP EARTHING: Construction: Copper or GI strip (25mm×1.6mm or 32mm×6mm) buried horizontally in a trench (0.5m deep). Used around large buildings, substations. Low resistance achieved over large area. 5. SYSTEM EARTHING (TN / TT / IT Systems): TN system: Neutral point of transformer directly earthed. TT system: Separate earth electrodes at source and consumer. IT system: No direct earth connection (isolated neutral) — used in hospitals. Requirements for Good Earthing: • Earth resistance should be < 1Ω (for power systems) • Minimum conductor size: 16mm² copper / 25mm² GI • Earth continuity conductor must not be fused or switched • Regular testing every 6 months
✅
Solar Plant: PV Panels→MPPT Charge Controller→Inverter→Transformer→Grid. Earthing types: Plate, Pipe (most common), Rod, Strip, and System earthing — all provide low-resistance path to earth to protect persons and equipment.
